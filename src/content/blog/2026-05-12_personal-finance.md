## The problem

![Everybody rents](/assets/everybody-rents.png)

You have to "pay rent" even if you lose your job, even when you get in a car crash, even if you have kids, even when you retire.

I want this guide to help you save (in the literal safety sense), spend wisely, and make more money in the long term. We'll set up a system so your income is automatically put in the right places so you're free to spend on what you love, guilt-free. [^sources]

[^sources]: These aren't new ideas: many personal finance books, channels, and blogs describe the same framework. Books like [The Simple Path to Wealth](https://a.co/d/0ivcUUqW) and [I Will Teach You To Be Rich](https://a.co/d/06TZkn2k), blog posts from [Mr. Money Mustache](https://www.mrmoneymustache.com/) and [from Vanguard](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/what_to_do_with_your_next_dollar.pdf), or from financial experts like [Warren Buffett](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2013ltr.pdf) and [Ben Felix](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCDXTQ8nWmx_EhZ2v-kp7QxA). But few seem to know about it, let alone follow it.

![Flowing money system](/assets/flowing-money-system.png)

Let's build it piece by piece.

First, this assumes you have a checking account. If not, [open one with SoFi](https://www.sofi.com/banking/checking-account/).

## Step 2: spend more on happiness

Money can buy some happiness. Stolen from [Dan Pink](https://youtu.be/iGkVqeFUVNU), think about these questions before spending:

1. Does this buy me time or steal it?
2. Is this a story or just a thing?
3. Does this bring me closer to people or is it only for me?
4. Is this a treat or a new standard?
5. Am I paying now to enjoy later or paying as I go?

These apply to everyone. But everyone has different settings on these "money dials" (stolen from [Ramit Sethi](https://www.iwillteachyoutoberich.com/money-dials/)):

| Money dial       | Examples                                                 |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Convenience      | Food delivery, house cleaners, personal assistant        |
| Travel           | Weekend trip, first-class flights, whole month abroad    |
| Health / fitness | Gym membership, personal trainer, private chef           |
| Experiences      | Concerts, VIP tickets, Michelin-star meals               |
| Freedom          | Emergency fund, negotiate remote work, take a sabbatical |
| Relationships    | Host dinner, pay for family vacation, build guest space  |
| Generosity       | Buy a friend’s coffee, 100% tips, pay off someone’s debt |
| Luxury           | Nice bedsheets, a $10k Eames chair, premium car          |
| Social status    | Designer bag, tailored clothes, private club membership  |
| Self-improvement | Books, private coach, personal retreat                   |

Pick one or two that matter most to you, and turn them way up. Spend four or even ten times more here. Get creative. But also rank all of them and turn the bottom five way down.

For me, I love experiences and will spend thousands on a guided ski trip or hundreds on an incredible meal with friends. But I don't care about luxury or social status, so I choose cheap stays when traveling and keep a small, non-designer wardrobe.

Another tip: each year, consider paying for something that you'll cherish for the next decade.

### Step 2.1: get a credit card

![Pick a card, any card](/assets/pick-a-card-any-card.png)
_Pick a card, any card... Actually don't. Most of them are trash._

You're going to be spending, so you might as well get free rewards.

A credit card basically gives you free short-term loans with free rewards, but **you must** pay them off every month. Different cards have different rewards. Some are simple, some are complicated. Some charge fees, which might be worth it [^my-cards].

[^my-cards]: I like to optimize and I've done some math to pick my cards. I use the [Citi / Costco Anywhere Visa](https://www.citi.com/credit-cards/citi-costco-anywhere-visa-credit-card) for gas, I spend enough on rent and dining that the [Bilt Obsidian](https://www.bilt.com/card) card is worth the fee, and enough on groceries for the [American Express Blue Cash Preferred](https://www.americanexpress.com/us/credit-cards/card/blue-cash-preferred/). Everything else goes to the [Fidelity Visa](https://www.fidelity.com/spend-save/preapproval-visa-signature-card). But all this only gets me a couple more hundred dollars in value than a flat cashback card.

Keep it simple and pick [this one from Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/spend-save/visa-signature-card). I have one too. It's Visa so it's accepted everywhere, has no fees, and gives 2% cash back. Just click "Apply Now". If you need to open an account, select "Cash management". If you don't qualify for some reason, try [Wells Fargo Active Cash](https://creditcards.wellsfargo.com/active-cash-credit-card) or [Citi Double Cash](https://www.citi.com/credit-cards/citi-double-cash-credit-card) (but they charge foreign transaction fees), the [Capital One Quicksilver](https://www.capitalone.com/credit-cards/quicksilver/) (but that only rewards 1.5% cash back), or the [Chase Freedom Unlimited](https://creditcards.chase.com/cash-back-credit-cards/freedom/unlimited) (but that suffers both problems).

Once the card is open, set up autopay to ensure you pay off the balance every month.

## Step 3: avoid debt

Again, you must settle your credit card balance every month. If you don't, that free loan becomes a high-interest loan and quickly grows every month you don't pay.

If you do have existing loans, don't worry, we'll tackle them in the next section.

Only take future loans strategically, where you plausibly expect to pay it back and earn more, like a university degree, trade school certificate, to start a business, or a permanent home. Not for funding vacations, buying things, or taking risky investments.

## Step 4: build a one-month emergency fund

Before going further, we need to ensure that if you lose your job or something bad happens, you can make it a month without taking on credit card debt.

If you don't have one already, open a savings account. Use SoFi if you opened a checking account there. Or consider [Ally](https://www.ally.com/bank/online-savings-account/). But any reputable, "FDIC insured" bank works fine.

Then, on their website, find an option for automatic contributions and deposit 100% of your surplus. Let that run each month until you've reached an amount equal to one month of expenses.

Congratulations, now you're not "living paycheck to paycheck"!

## Quick detour: what are stocks?

Before investing, it's important to understand what's happening conceptually.

### Investment accelerates growth

1. Sofia starts her own company, Burrito-Bike, using her own bike and kitchen, so she can only deliver burritos in her neighborhood.

1. Months later, her friend Leo sees her doing well and wants to help her expand. They agree the value of the company is $10,000 and Leo invests $2,000 for 20% ownership. Sofia uses that to buy more bikes and hire help, expanding to three more neighborhoods.

1. Leo connects with a couple private investor friends, who believe Sofia can grow the company further. The investors agree the total value is $1 million and buy 20% of the company for $200,000. Sofia and Leo now own smaller percentages of a larger business. Leo's 20% becomes 16%, worth $160,000. But he would have to find a buyer to turn that value into cash (to "liquidate" it).

1. Years later, Burrito-Bike needs more funding and some private investors also want to sell part of their stake. So the company "goes public" through an "initial public offering" (IPO). It follows a formal process to publish detailed financials and set an initial stock price.

1. Now the public can buy and sell Burrito-Bike stock, which means "the market" determines the price. If buyers are willing to pay more, the price rises. If sellers accept less, the price falls.

So investment accelerates growth. And in a loose sense, the market "votes" with money: companies that attract buyers can raise more money and use it to grow.

### That can fund retirement

The US, recognizing that the stock market can fund companies and help people build wealth while relying less on Social Security, encourages the public to invest. People can open "individual retirement accounts" (IRAs). Employees can use 401(k) accounts. People with "high-deductible health plans" (HDHPs) can open "health savings accounts" (HSAs). Each is taxed less than a normal investment account.

Here's how this might play out:

> A worker, Nora, puts part of each paycheck into her 401(k) account. Her employer may add extra money too. Nora can use that account to buy stocks or other investments. Nora could invest in Burrito-Bike. But what if their competitor Taco-Scooter ends up delivering better food faster and overtaking their business? Nora cannot predict this. Instead, Nora can invest in both. Now if either wins, Nora wins. She does not need to pick the winner, she just needs the food delivery market to do well.

This is the principle behind "index funds". If you buy a slice of every company in the world [^not-all], you don't need to pick the winner, you just need the economy to do well in the long term.

[^not-all]: Not literally all companies. In 1957, S&P began tracking 500 large US companies as a rough picture of "the market". Later, Vanguard founder John Bogle noticed that even professional investors were not reliably beating the market especially after considering their costs. So he created a low-cost fund that tried to "own the market" instead of outsmarting it.

## Step 5: invest the rest

Because of inflation, cash is always losing value so you can't just keep it under the mattress. Savings accounts are better but not ideal. If we take a bit more _risk_, we can _expect_ better growth. So from here, it's rather simple: we're going to put your surplus income where it has the highest expected return.

Notice that paying down an 8% interest loan is functionally equivalent to a guaranteed 8% return on investment. Realizing this, we can sort where to put our surplus money. Here's the prioritized list. Each is like a bucket of money, and we want to "fill bucket #1" before "bucket #2", etc.:

1.  Maximize HSA and 401(k) matches. It's free money!

    If you're young and healthy, you're probably not going to the doctor often, and should consider a high-deductible health plan if offered, which qualifies you for an HSA. The US government does not tax deposits, growth, or withdrawals for health expenses. But after 65, you can withdraw without penalty, so it's like a secret retirement account. When you're young, treat the HSA like an investment account. When you're getting older, treat it like a healthcare reserve.

    Your employer might say this:

    > We match 50% of your HSA contributions up to $500 per year.

    This is an immediate, guaranteed 50% return. Your employer will offer a way to configure the amount, which will be taken directly from your paycheck, and you should set it to `$500 / number of paychecks left this year`.

    Sometimes they don't match and just give free money:

    > We contribute $500 per year to your HSA if you enroll in the HSA-eligible health plan.

    If they say something like this, you don't have to do anything!

    401(k)s are similar, but have different tax benefits. You can only withdraw once you turn 59½ [^penalty]. There are two kinds: a "traditional" one is taxed when you withdraw and a "Roth" is taxed when you deposit. Generally, pick traditional if you expect your tax rate to be lower later and Roth if you expect it to be higher. Do a 50/50 split if you're not sure.

    [^penalty]: Technically you can withdraw before 59½, but the government charges a 10% penalty in most cases.

    Commonly, your employer will partner with a brokerage like Vanguard or Charles Schwab and offer a login. Use that, find an automated transfer option, and enter in that value. It'll automatically come out of your next paycheck [^next-paycheck].

    [^next-paycheck]: They often require 5 business days notice or it'll actually be your paycheck after next, so do it far enough ahead of time!

    They might say something like this:

    > We match 50% of your contributions up to 5% of eligible compensation.

    So if your income is $50,000, then they'll match 50% of $2,500. Your employer will offer a way to configure your contribution, which will be taken directly from your paycheck, and you should set it to `$2,500 / number of paychecks left this year`.

1.  Pay down any debt with an 8% or higher interest rate.

1.  Expand the emergency fund

    You've gotten the big wins and paid off the big debts, so it's fair to boost preparedness. Increase to at least three months of expenses. If your job is at-risk or you have dependents, go to at least six months.

    There's probably no need to go beyond this amount. Remember that this is like holding cash. This money will not grow much, but you can get it [when you need it](#step-oh-shit-i-need-money).

1.  Pay down any debt with a 6% or higher interest rate.

1.  Maximize tax-advantaged investments: HSA, 401(k), and IRA.

    You already maximized _employer matches_ on the HSA and 401(k), but you can contribute more yourself.

    Next, open an IRA if you qualify, probably with Fidelity. IRAs are also available in traditional and Roth flavors.

    Since these three types of accounts are "tax-advantaged", the government sets a yearly maximum contribution. For 2026, HSA is $4,400, 401(k) is $24,500, and IRA is $7,500.

    Spread your contributions over the rest of the year. If you've already contributed $4,500 to your 401(k) and have 20 more paychecks this year, then you want to contribute `($24,500 - $4,500) / 20 = $1,000` per paycheck.

    For each of these tax-advantaged accounts, invest in a "target-date fund" set for the year you turn 601

1.  Pay down any debt with a 4% or higher interest 1ate.

1.  Put the rest in a taxable investment account.

    Now we'll set up our own brokerage account. We'll be able to sell and withdraw at any time, but will owe _capital gains_ taxes. Fidelity, Vanguard, and Wealthfront are good options. Once opened, find a way to automatically buy [VT](https://www.google.com/finance/quote/VT:NYSEARCA), an index fund that represents the world economy.

Notice we're not paying off lower debts other than their monthly minimums. You can expect taxable investments to grow _faster than your debt will_ over long periods, so you'll have more money in the end.

I included some extra "steps" you're sure to come across below, too, though:

## Step "I feel a big purchase coming"

Use another savings account to save for a big purchase like a car or a down payment on a house or a wedding.

For these big purchases, always negotiate. If you spend just an hour preparing for a negotiation and that saves you $1,000 on your car purchase, you were just effectively paid $1,000/hour.

I also like the idea, say, of auto-saving $150 per month to have a $1,800 per year vacation budget, or $50 per month to have a $600 "gifts for friends and family" budget.

## Step "oh shit I need money"

If you need money try these first:

1. Use your emergency fund
2. Use other savings accounts
3. Earn additional money (raise, side gig)
4. Ask family and friends for money
5. Withdraw the _principal_ from your IRA
6. Go into credit card debt

## Step "I wanna gamble"

My take: wait until you have $10,000 in the prescribed low-risk investments, then you can feel free to invest just 5% of your net worth into hand-picked stocks. But think of it as entertainment. Know that you're gambling, so it better be money you're okay completely losing.

## Step 🧓: retirement

You've watched your portfolio grow to a big enough amount™!

Notice you didn't have to do anything: everyone else was working hard and inventing new technologies that increase productivity and thus "the economy", using your invested money, so those rewards come back to you!

Using the "4% rule", we can estimate how much wealth you need to accumulate to retire. For example, if your fixed expenses are $3,333 per month, then you can effectively retire with a $1 million portfolio [^future-expenses].

[^future-expenses]: Maybe slow your roll though. That $3,333 might balloon to $9,000 with a mortgage payment and a couple kids.
